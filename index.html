A proposal for mental health intake

Most intakes ask how you feel. Few ask what happened to you.

Many people can't name their own symptoms. A trained clinician can recognize the patterns in a person's history. Root Intake is an intake pathway that asks about life events and circumstances, so dissociative identity disorder (DID) and other complex trauma disorders are found sooner.

The pathway: Ask, Train, Connect

Three parts that change the first thirty minutes of care. No new specialty clinic required.

1

Ask

A short web-based intake asks about life events and circumstances, along with symptoms. Questions are mostly multiple choice, with everyday scenarios and optional spaces to add more.

2

Train

Clinicians learn to recognize when a trauma history fits the pattern of a complex trauma or dissociative disorder, then confirm with a structured interview such as the DDIS or SCID-D.

3

Connect

A positive assessment leads to a warm handoff: a dissociation-informed clinician, a safety plan and resources. A patient advisory group helps shape the pathway.

For clients

An intake that doesn't wear you out

Long paperwork can become a reason to stop seeking care. This intake is built to be easier to finish.

  • Mostly multiple-choice questions, instead of pages of 1-to-10 scales.
  • Everyday scenarios to choose from, so you don't have to find the words on the spot.
  • Optional spaces to add more, only when you want to.
  • Questions about your life and circumstances, along with how you feel.
  • You can skip any question. Your answers are never a diagnosis on their own.

For clinicians

A two-hour online module

Researchers describe a "dearth of training" on dissociative disorders (Brand et al., 2019). The proposed module, refreshed yearly, covers:

  1. How dissociation presents, including memory gaps, lost time and detachment
  2. Reading a trauma history for patterns linked to complex disorders
  3. Telling DID apart from PTSD, borderline personality disorder and psychosis
  4. Asking without leading, and not pressing for details before a client is stable

Why this exists

Built from twenty years on the other side of intake

Root Intake was designed by Gabriella Wells, a psychology student training to become a trauma counselor in rural Southern Oregon. The intake and training are online, so they can reach small towns where specialists are scarce.

The research behind it

American Journal of Psychiatry, 2006

Foote et al.

Interviewed 82 outpatients. 29% had a dissociative disorder and 6% had DID. Only 5% had ever been diagnosed. Those patients reported far more childhood abuse.

Psychiatry Research, 2007

Şar, Akyüz & Doğan

Surveyed 628 women in their homes. 1.1% had DID. Childhood abuse and neglect predicted a diagnosis. Most had never been treated.

Journal of Traumatic Stress, 2019

Brand et al.

An online program for patients and their therapists led to improvement at one and two years. The authors call for wider clinician training.

Why it matters: in one clinic, having a dissociative disorder was the strongest predictor of repeated suicide attempts (Foote et al., 2008). Earlier recognition can save lives.

Most researchers link DID to severe childhood trauma. Some argue that suggestion and media can shape symptoms. Careful structured interviews help clinicians tell the difference.

A twelve-month pilot would track how many people finish the intake, how many receive a structured interview, and their experience. A larger study would then compare clinics using the pathway with clinics using their usual intake on time to an accurate diagnosis.
